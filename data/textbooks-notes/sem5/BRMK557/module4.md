## Module 4: Copyrights & Trademarks

### Copyright: Definition and Nature

- **Copyright** is the exclusive legal right granted to creators of original literary, artistic, musical, and dramatic works (and sound recordings, films, software) to reproduce, distribute, perform, display, and adapt their work for a limited period.
- **Governing law**: Copyright Act, 1957 (India), amended 1994, 1999, 2012; administered by the **Copyright Office, New Delhi** (Registrar of Copyrights) under DPIIT.
- **Key principle**: Copyright protects the **expression of an idea, not the idea itself** (idea-expression dichotomy). Two people can write the same plot idea independently — each owns their own expression.
- **Nature of copyright**: automatic — protection arises **on creation**, no registration is compulsory (though registration creates public record and prima facie evidence in infringement suits).
- **What is required for protection**: **Originality** (the work must originate from the author, not be copied — India follows the "skill and judgment" standard, not the high US "creativity" threshold) and **fixation** (work must be expressed in a tangible form).
- **Copyright vs patent**: copyright protects creative expression (no novelty requirement, long term); patent protects technical inventions (novelty required, 20 years, registration mandatory).

### Copyright Classes (Works Protected)

- **Classes of works under Sec 13, Copyright Act 1957** (PYQ 2023 Q7a — know all):
  1. **Literary works**: books, journals, research papers, computer programs (software source and object code), compilations, databases, tables. (In India, "literary work" includes **computer programmes** — Sec 2(o).)
  2. **Musical works**: compositions — the melody/score, not the lyrics; copyright in a song's music is distinct from the lyric's.
  3. **Dramatic works**: plays, screenplays, scripts — including choreographic shows and mime.
  4. **Artistic works**: paintings, sculptures, drawings, photographs, architectural plans, graphic works — works of visual art irrespective of artistic quality.
  5. **Cinematograph films**: films, video recordings (protected as works distinct from the underlying script/music).
  6. **Sound recordings**: audio recordings (records, CDs, digital audio) — distinct copyright from the musical/literary work recorded.
- **Related (neighbouring) rights**: performer's rights (singers, actors) and broadcaster's rights — protected separately under the 1994 amendment.
- Each class can carry multiple overlapping rights: a song = musical work (music) + literary work (lyrics) + sound recording (the recording) — three separate copyrights.

### Author Rights (Economic and Moral Rights)

- **Author**: the person who creates the work. For a computer-generated work, the author is the person who causes the creation. For a work created during employment, the **employer is the first owner** (work made for hire), unless otherwise agreed.
- **Economic rights (exclusive rights of the owner)** — Sec 14:
  - Reproduction/right to copy.
  - Issue of copies to the public (distribution).
  - Performance in public (plays, concerts, recitals).
  - Communication to the public (broadcast, streaming, internet).
  - Translation and adaptation (making derivative works).
  - Rental rights (for computer programs, films, sound recordings).
- **Moral rights (Sec 57 — author's personal rights, non-transferable, survive even after assignment of economic rights)**:
  - **Right of paternity**: right to claim authorship and be named (or to prevent being named).
  - **Right of integrity**: right to restrain distortion, mutilation, or modification of the work that damages the author's reputation.
- Moral rights remain with the author even when economic rights are sold/assigned — a frequently tested point (example: author can stop a publisher's mutilating edits even after selling copyright).
- **Assignment vs licence**: assignment = transfer of ownership (must be in writing, signed, with duration/territory); licence = permission to use (exclusive or non-exclusive).

### Term of Copyright

- **General rule**: **lifetime of the author + 60 years** from the beginning of the calendar year following the author's death (PYQ: term of copyright).
- **Special terms** (India):
  - Joint work: life of the last surviving author + 60 years.
  - Anonymous/pseudonymous works: 60 years from publication.
  - Cinematograph films, sound recordings, photographs: 60 years from publication (or from making, if not published within 50 years of making).
  - Government works, public undertakings: 60 years from publication.
  - **Broadcast reproduction right: 25 years; performer's rights: 50 years.**
- Term starts from the year *following* the event (e.g., author dies in 2020 → protection till end of 2080).
- **Public domain**: after expiry, works can be freely used by anyone (Shakespeare's plays, classical music, old films).

### Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

- **Infringement (Sec 51)**: doing any act (reproduction, communication, adaptation, etc.) that only the copyright owner may do, without a licence; also includes importing infringing copies, permitting premises for infringing performances, and selling/distributing infringing copies.
- **Infringement tests**: substantial similarity (quantitative + qualitative — even a small but important extract can infringe) + copying (access + similarity).
- **Remedies**: civil (injunction, damages/accounts, delivery of infringing copies), criminal (imprisonment up to 3 years + fine, on complaint), and administrative (border control — customs seizure of infringing imports).
- **Fair Use / Fair Dealing doctrine** (PYQ 2024 Q7a — must-know):
  - **Fair dealing** (Indian statutory wording, Sec 52): certain acts are **not** infringement:
  - **Research and private use**: fair dealing with a literary, dramatic, musical, or artistic work for the purposes of **research or private study**.
  - **Criticism or review**: fair dealing for criticism/review of a work or other works.
  - **Reporting current events** (newspapers, periodicals, broadcast).
  - **Judicial proceedings** and professional advice by legal practitioners.
  - Reproducing a work for **educational purposes** by a teacher or student in the course of instruction (classroom use).
  - Storing a work in an electronic medium for research/private study (with conditions).
- **Fair use factors (US doctrine — four-factor test, useful comparison)**:
  1. Purpose and character of the use (commercial vs non-profit/educational; transformative use favours fairness).
  2. Nature of the copyrighted work (factual works get more latitude than creative works).
  3. Amount and substantiality of the portion used (quantity + importance).
  4. Effect on the potential market for/value of the work.
- **Educational fair use in research** (exam asks "what constitutes educational fair use"):
  - Using short excerpts with attribution for teaching, criticism, review, or research.
  - Non-commercial, non-repetitive use that does not substitute for the original.
  - Quoting with proper citation; use of figures/tables from papers with credit.
  - NOT fair: reproducing an entire book/chapter as course pack for sale, downloading and redistributing copyrighted software/music, using copyrighted images in a commercial product, systematic copying of journals.
- **India vs US note**: India uses the closed-list "fair dealing" (statutory exceptions in Sec 52) rather than the open-ended US "fair use" — but courts interpret research/private study liberally.
- **Important 2016 Delhi HC case (for discussion)**: *The Chancellor, Masters and Scholars of the University of Oxford v. Rameshwari Photocopy Services* — photocopying excerpts (course packs) for educational purposes by Delhi University held to be fair dealing; education exception is broad.

### Copyright Registration in India

- Registration is **optional** but recommended — creates a public record and gives prima facie evidence in disputes.
- **Steps**:
  1. File Form XIV with the Copyright Office (online via copyright.gov.in) — details of the work, author, claimant.
  2. Pay the prescribed fee (varies by work type; software registration is fee-exempt in India).
  3. Examination and scrutiny by the Registrar; objections to be heard.
  4. **Registration certificate** issued; entered in the Register of Copyrights.
- **Software copyright**: source code deposited; no separate registration fee for software.
- **Disputes**: Copyright Office provides compulsory licences, and infringement suits go to District Court/High Court (Commercial Courts).

### Trademarks: Definition and Eligibility

- **Trademark**: a mark capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one person from those of others — includes words, names, symbols, logos, slogans, shapes, sounds, colours, letters, numerals, or combinations thereof (Sec 2(1)(zb), Trade Marks Act 1999).
- **Governing law**: Trade Marks Act, 1999 (India); administered by the **Trade Marks Registry** under CGPDTM.
- **Functions of a trademark**: source identification (who made it), quality guarantee (consistent expectation), advertising/marketing vehicle, and goodwill protection.
- **Eligibility criteria for registration** (PYQ 2023 Q7b):
  1. **Distinctive (capable of distinguishing)**: the mark must be able to identify and distinguish goods/services (inherently distinctive or acquired distinctiveness through use).
  2. **Graphical representation**: the mark must be capable of being represented graphically (paper/electronic).
  3. **Not deceptive or confusing**.
  4. **Not identical/similar to an earlier registered or pending mark** for same/similar goods (no conflict).
  5. Not a prohibited mark (see below).
- **Absolute grounds for refusal (Sec 9)**: devoid of distinctive character; descriptive of goods/services (quality, kind, quantity, purpose, value, geographical origin); customary in trade; deceptive; contrary to law/morality; scandalous; confusingly similar to well-known marks; shape resulting from the nature of goods or giving technical result.
- **Relative grounds (Sec 11)**: identical/similar to an earlier mark for identical/similar goods creating likelihood of confusion or unfair advantage.
- **Well-known trademarks**: recognized globally (e.g., Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Tata) — entitled to protection even without registration in India; no likelihood-of-confusion requirement.

### Types of Marks

- **Trade mark**: for goods (e.g., Parachute for coconut oil).
- **Service mark**: for services (e.g., Airtel, Café Coffee Day) — note India's Act covers both under "trademark" with the service mark being the service variant.
- **Collective mark**: used by members of a collective/association (e.g., trade unions, cooperative societies) to indicate membership — e.g., "IFFCO" for a cooperative of fertilizer farmers.
- **Certification mark**: certifies quality, origin, material, or mode of manufacture — e.g., ISI mark, Agmark, Woolmark (registered to a certifying body, not used by it in trade).
- **Others in practice**: word marks, device/logos, shape marks (Coca-Cola bottle), sound marks (MGM lion roar, Yahoo yodel — India allows sound marks), colour marks, slogan marks, and **3D marks**.

### Trademark Registration Process (India)

```
[DIAGRAM: Trademark registration in India
Search (pre-filing) --> File application (Form TM-A, ipindia.gov.in)
  --> Examination by Registry (absolute + relative grounds)
  --> Show cause hearing if objections --> Publication in TM Journal
  --> Opposition period (4 months, any third party) --> Opposition hearing if any
  --> Registration + Certificate --> Term 10 years --> Renewal (every 10 yrs)
Priority date = application filing date; "TM" vs ® usage rules apply
]
```

- **Key steps** (PYQ 2023 Q7b):
  1. **Pre-filing search** in the TM Registry database (IP India) to check conflicts.
  2. **File application (Form TM-A)**: online, single application for multi-class (one application can cover multiple classes) — Indian system is single-application-multi-class; goods/services classified under **Nice Classification (45 classes)**.
  3. **Examination**: Registry examines for absolute (Sec 9) and relative (Sec 11) grounds; issues examination report.
  4. **Response/hearing** to objections, if any.
  5. **Publication** in the Trade Marks Journal (mandatory).
  6. **Opposition** by third parties within **4 months** of publication (Sec 21).
  7. **Registration and certificate**; term **10 years from filing date**, renewable indefinitely for 10-year periods.
- **Usage requirement**: If a registered mark is not used for **5 continuous years**, it becomes vulnerable to removal for non-use.
- **Symbols**: ™ (applied/pending), ® (registered — using ® on an unregistered mark is an offence), ℠ (service mark, rarely used).
- **Well-known mark protection** extends to dissimilar goods (dilution protection).
- **Madrid Protocol** (PYQ-relevant): international trademark system administered by WIPO — a single application (in a member country) extends protection to many member countries (designating countries); India joined the Madrid Protocol in **2013** (Madrid Protocol on International Registration of Marks); Indian applicants use Form MM2 to file international applications through the Indian Registry, which verifies and forwards to WIPO (IB — International Bureau).

### Trademark Infringement and Passing Off

- **Infringement** (PYQ 2024 Q7b — direct question): use of an identical/deceptively similar mark **in relation to identical/similar goods or services** for which the mark is **registered**, creating likelihood of confusion — Sec 29. Only a **registered** mark owner can sue for infringement.
  - Also includes: using the mark on packaging, in advertising, as domain name, on similar goods with reputation causing unfair advantage/detriment (dilution of well-known marks).
- **Passing off**: common-law remedy (tort) for **unregistered** marks — a trader misrepresents (directly or by "look-alike" packaging) that their goods are those of another, harming the goodwill of the earlier user. No registration required.
- Comparison table (exam favorite):

| Basis | Infringement | Passing Off |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Statutory basis | Trade Marks Act 1999, Sec 29 | Common law (tort of deceit) |
| Mark status | Must be registered | Unregistered is enough (prior use + goodwill) |
| Need to prove goodwill | No — rights flow from registration | Yes — plaintiff must prove goodwill/reputation |
| Need to prove confusion | Presumed for identical marks on identical goods | Must prove likelihood of deception/confusion |
| Remedy | Injunction, damages, accounts, delivery-up | Injunction, damages, accounts |

- **Test for passing off (classical trinity)**: (1) goodwill/reputation of the plaintiff, (2) misrepresentation by the defendant leading to deception, (3) actual or probable damage to the plaintiff.
- **Famous Indian cases**: *Parle's "Glucovita" vs "Glucocid"* (deceptive similarity), *Daimler Chrysler vs DRB* ("BENZ"), *Havells vs* look-alike cases, *McDonald's vs Mac-style* local restaurants.
- **Domain name disputes**: often resolved via passing off + WIPO UDRP arbitration.

### Quick Revision Points (Module 4)

- Copyright = expression, not idea; automatic on creation; literary/musical/dramatic/artistic/film/sound classes (know Sec 13 list).
- Term = life + 60 years (60 from publication for films/recordings/photos); performer 50, broadcast 25.
- Moral rights (paternity + integrity) survive assignment — Sec 57.
- Fair dealing exceptions under Sec 52: research/private study, criticism/review, news reporting, education — plus US 4-factor fair use test.
- Registration optional; TM Act 1999, term 10 years renewable; Nice classes; Madrid Protocol (2013 for India).
- Infringement (registered, statutory) vs passing off (unregistered, common law): goodwill, misrepresentation, damage.
