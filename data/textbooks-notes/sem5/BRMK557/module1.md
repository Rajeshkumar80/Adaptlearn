## Module 1: Introduction to Research & Research Ethics

### Engineering Research: Meaning and Objectives

- **Research** (re + search): A systematic, objective, and organized process of inquiry aimed at discovering new facts, verifying existing knowledge, or developing new theories, products, or solutions.
- **Engineering research** is research applied to engineering problems — it combines scientific principles with practical design constraints (cost, safety, reliability, sustainability) to produce new knowledge, devices, processes, or systems.
- **Key characteristics of research**: Systematic (follows a defined procedure), Logical (based on reasoning), Empirical (grounded in observation/experiment), Replicable (results can be verified by others), Objective (free from personal bias), and Cyclical (findings lead to new questions).
- **Objectives of engineering research** (what a researcher tries to achieve):
  - To gain new knowledge/familiarity with a phenomenon (exploratory objective).
  - To describe characteristics of a system or population accurately (descriptive objective).
  - To determine frequency or regularity of occurrence of events (diagnostic objective).
  - To test hypotheses and establish cause-effect relationships (explanatory/analytical objective).
- **Purpose of research** from the researcher's angle: to earn a degree, solve a real problem, gain intellectual satisfaction, serve society, or improve engineering practice.
- Good engineering research must be **reproducible and verifiable** — a result that cannot be reproduced by others is not research, it is an artifact.

### Motivation in Research

- **Motivation** is the internal drive that pushes a researcher to undertake and persist with an investigation. Common motivating factors:
  - Desire for degree or academic advancement (degree motivation).
  - Desire to solve practical/industrial problems (problem-solving motivation).
  - Intellectual curiosity and desire for discovery.
  - Career growth — publications, patents, and recognition.
  - Social responsibility — contributing to national development, safety, sustainability.
  - Financial incentives — consultancy, funding, grants (e.g., SERB, DST, Visvesvaraya scheme).
- **Intrinsic motivation** (curiosity, passion) produces more sustained, higher-quality research than **extrinsic motivation** (reward, fear) — exam-relevant point for "why do researchers quit".
- Motivation directly influences research quality: poorly motivated research often leads to shortcuts and misconduct (ties into ethics module).

### Types of Engineering Research

- **Fundamental (Basic/Pure) Research**: Aims at expanding the frontiers of knowledge without an immediate practical application. Example: understanding the quantum behavior of a semiconductor material. Outcome: new theories, principles, laws. Usually university-driven, published as papers.
- **Applied Research**: Uses fundamental knowledge to solve a specific practical problem. Example: designing a solar cell using the semiconductor theory above. Outcome: new products, processes, prototypes.
- **Experimental Research**: Knowledge gained by conducting controlled experiments — varying independent variables and measuring dependent variables. The backbone of most engineering research; includes design of experiments (DoE), control groups, and statistical analysis of results.
- **Theoretical (Analytical) Research**: Develops mathematical models, proofs, simulations, or frameworks without physical experimentation. Example: deriving a new control algorithm and proving its stability mathematically.
- **Other classifications**: Quantitative vs Qualitative; Descriptive vs Analytical; Conceptual vs Empirical; and **Exploratory vs Explanatory** research.
- Comparison table:

| Basis | Fundamental | Applied | Experimental | Theoretical |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Aim | New knowledge for its own sake | Solve practical problem | Empirical verification | Model/formulation |
| Output | Theories, principles | Products, solutions | Data, verified facts | Equations, models, proofs |
| Timeframe | Long, open-ended | Short, goal-driven | Medium | Medium |
| Example | Heat-transfer physics | Better heat exchanger | Lab test of exchanger | CFD simulation of exchanger |

### Identifying and Formulating a Research Problem

- **Research problem**: A question or gap in existing knowledge that the researcher wants to answer. "A research problem is an unsatisfactory situation that needs a solution."
- **Sources of research problems**: Literature gaps, industrial problems, unexplained phenomena, prior work suggestions ("future scope"), societal needs, conferences/workshops, personal observation.
- **Criteria for a good research problem** (exam favorite): Significance (worth solving), Feasibility (resources, time, skills), Novelty (not already solved), Unambiguous (clearly defined scope), and Ethical acceptability.
- **Steps in problem identification and formulation** (PYQ 2024 Q1a):
  1. Identify a broad area of interest.
  2. Survey literature and prior work to find the gap.
  3. Narrow the broad area to a specific, researchable problem.
  4. State the problem in a clear, testable form (problem statement).
  5. Define scope and objectives (what is included/excluded).
  6. Formulate hypotheses and research questions.
  7. Set boundary conditions, assumptions, and constraints.
  8. Define success criteria/metrics for evaluation.
- **Problem statement format** (commonly tested): "The problem is that [current state] is inadequate because [gap/limitation]. This research will [objective] using [approach], so that [expected benefit]."
- **Defining scope and objectives**: Scope = boundaries of the study (what will and will not be covered, limits on resources/time/domain); Objectives = measurable targets; they must be **SMART — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound**.
- **Common pitfalls**: problem too broad/vague, no measurable objective, no clear gap, infeasible scope, biased problem statement.

### Research Ethics in Engineering Practice

- **Ethics** = systematic principles of right and wrong conduct. Research ethics governs honesty, integrity, and responsibility in the entire research lifecycle — planning, data collection, analysis, publication, and review.
- **Why ethics matters in engineering research**: engineered products affect safety and lives; fabricated data can cause physical harm (e.g., false strength data for a bridge); unethical research destroys public trust and wastes public money.
- **Core ethical principles**:
  - Honesty (report data truthfully, no fabrication).
  - Integrity (keep promises, consistent standards).
  - Objectivity (avoid bias in design, analysis, interpretation).
  - Carefulness (avoid careless errors, keep good records).
  - Openness (share data, methods, results).
  - Respect for intellectual property (no plagiarism, credit others).
  - Confidentiality (protect sponsors, reviewers, unpublished data).
  - Responsible publication (publish only novel work, no duplicate publication).
  - Legality (obey laws and institutional policies).
  - Human subject protection (informed consent, privacy, no harm) — relevant to engineering research involving user studies.

### Research Misconduct: Fabrication, Falsification, and Plagiarism (FFP)

- **Fabrication**: Making up data or results and recording/reporting them as real. Example: inventing experimental readings that were never taken; generating fake simulation outputs.
- **Falsification**: Manipulating research materials, equipment, processes, or omitting/deleting data such that results are misrepresented. Example: cropping out an outlier that contradicts the hypothesis; adjusting time axes; "beautifying" plots (image manipulation).
- **Plagiarism**: Using another's ideas, words, data, or work without proper acknowledgement. Types: direct (copy-paste), self-plagiarism (reusing own published work without citation), mosaic/patchwork (rewording while keeping structure), and accidental plagiarism.
- **FFP is the "3 cardinal sins" of research** — these are considered *research misconduct* by most funding agencies (e.g., DST, UGC). Office of Research Integrity (ORI), USA, defines exactly these three.
- **Other questionable practices** (beyond FFP): data manipulation, duplicate publication, salami slicing (splitting one study into many thin papers), p-hacking (fishing for significant p-values), citation manipulation, and authorship abuse.
- **Consequences of misconduct**: retraction of papers, loss of degree/position, ban from funding, legal action, loss of institutional reputation, and harm to society.
- **How to avoid plagiarism**: cite every source, use quotation marks for verbatim text, paraphrase properly, keep track of references, and run plagiarism checks before submission.

### Plagiarism Check Tools

- **Turnitin**: Cloud-based similarity detection used by universities; compares submissions against a huge database of web pages, academic papers, and student papers; produces a **Similarity Index (percentage)** and color-coded report. Uses a "text-matching" approach (does not judge intent or ethics — it only flags matches).
- **iThenticate**: Similar engine by Turnitin, aimed at publishers, journals, and researchers for pre-submission manuscript checking; the standard in journal peer review (IEEE, Elsevier, Springer use it).
- **Other tools**: Urkund/Ouriginal, Grammarly plagiarism checker, Copyscape, Dupli Checker, Dr.  Antiplagiat.
- **Interpreting similarity scores**: Most journals accept < 15-20% similarity with no single source exceeding ~1-2%; a high score to one source usually indicates plagiarism regardless of overall percentage. Similarity is *evidence*, not *verdict* — context matters.
- Exam point: Turnitin/iThenticate detect **text matching**, not fabrication or falsification — those need human scrutiny of data.

### Authorship Ethics and Conflicts of Interest

- **Authorship** is a statement of who is responsible for and contributed to the work. Authorship rules are given by **ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors)** and **COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics)**.
- **COPE principles (PYQ 2024 Q1b)**: transparency (authors must be honest about contributions), integrity, fairness (all who qualify get credit), and accountability (authors must correct errors and respond to critiques). COPE publishes guidelines, flowcharts, and case studies for handling misconduct.
- **ICMJE criteria for authorship** — all four must be met:
  1. Substantial contribution to conception/design or data acquisition/analysis.
  2. Drafting or critically revising the work.
  3. Final approval of the version to be published.
  4. Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work.
- **Ghost authorship**: someone who substantially contributed is *not* listed as author (often paid professional writers); unethical because it hides real contributors and can conceal industry influence.
- **Gift (honorary) authorship**: a person who contributed little or nothing is listed as author — usually to please a senior colleague, department head, or to inflate credentials. Unethical and dilutes responsibility.
- **Guest authorship** (related): prominent name added to increase paper acceptance chances.
- **Conflict of interest (COI)**: a situation where personal, financial, or professional interests could influence (or appear to influence) research judgment. Types: financial (shares in a company whose product is tested), personal (family members in project), academic (reviewing a competitor's paper), institutional.
- **COI handling**: disclose all interests at submission (conflict-of-interest statement), recuse from decisions/reviews when conflicted, and follow institutional policy. Transparency is the cure — the aim is to manage, not necessarily eliminate, conflicts.
- **Role of reviewers/editors**: reviewers must keep manuscripts confidential, avoid using unpublished data, and decline review if they have a COI.
