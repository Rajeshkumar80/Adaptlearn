## Module 5: Industrial Designs, Geographical Indications (GI) & Case Studies

### Industrial Design: Definition and Concept

- **Industrial design** (as IP) means only the **features of shape, configuration, pattern, ornament, or composition of lines or colours** applied to an article, whether in 2D or 3D, which appeal to and are judged solely by the **eye** — i.e., the *appearance* of an article (Sec 2(d), Designs Act 2000).
- **Key points**: Protection is for **aesthetic/visual appeal, not function or utility**. The design must be **applied to an article** and judged by the eye. A functional shape (e.g., a spanner's shape dictated by function) cannot be protected as a design.
- **Governing law**: The **Designs Act, 2000** and Designs Rules 2001 (replacing the 1911 Act); administered by the Designs Office under **CGPDTM**.
- **Difference from patent**: design protects appearance (no inventive step needed); patent protects technical function/solution.
- **Difference from copyright**: designs lose copyright protection once industrially applied — industrial designs must go to the Designs Act (Sec 15 Copyright Act: if a design is registered/registerable under Designs Act and applied more than 50 times industrially, copyright protection ceases).

### Registration Requirements under Designs Act 2000

- **Requirements for registration** (PYQ 2024 Q9a):
  1. **New or original**: the design must not be published in India or abroad, and not publicly used or disclosed before the filing (or before the priority date). It must not be identical to, or a significant imitation of, a known design.
  2. **Applies to an article**: it must be capable of being applied to a specific article (industrial application).
  3. **Judged solely by the eye**: visual appeal — no functionality; features dictated purely by function (or method/principle of construction) are not registrable.
  4. **No prior publication/use** anywhere in India or abroad.
  5. **Not contrary to public order or morality** (Sec 4).
- **Non-registrable designs (Sec 4)**: designs that are not new/original; not judged by the eye; mere mechanical devices (function-driven); mode/principle of construction; books, buildings, and layouts (structures); trademarks (covered by TM Act); integral parts of other articles where the final article's appearance alone matters.
- **Who can apply**: the creator (proprietor) or their assignee; the proprietor must be the first applicant.
- **Examination**: the Designs Office examines for novelty by searching the **designs database**; publication of accepted designs in the Patent/Design journal.

### Registration Process and Term

```
[DIAGRAM: Industrial design registration (India)
Conceive design --> Pre-filing search (designs database) --> File application
  (Form 1, with representations/photographs + statement of novelty)
  --> Examination by Designs Office (novelty + formalities)
  --> Objections/hearing if any --> Acceptance --> Registration
  --> Certificate --> Term: 10 years (from registration date, counted from filing)
  --> Extension: +5 years (renewal before expiry) --> Total max 15 years
]
```

- **Steps**:
  1. Search existing registered designs (IP India design database) for novelty.
  2. File application with **representations** (drawings/photographs showing the design) and a **statement of novelty** (clearly stating what is claimed as new).
  3. Examination: formal + substantive (novelty, eye-judged, article applicability).
  4. Objections → response/hearing if required.
  5. **Registration + certificate**; the design is then published.
  6. **Term of protection (PYQ 2024 Q9a — direct question)**: **10 years** from the date of registration (which is counted from the application date); renewable for a further **5 years** by paying extension fees **before expiry** — maximum protection **15 years** total.
- **Filing**: single application for one design on one article (or for sets of articles with a common design, e.g., a dinner set — "class of articles" provision); international filing possible via **Locarno Classification** (32 classes).
- **Infringement of design (Sec 22)**: piracy — applying a registered design or its fraudulent/imitation to an article, importing such articles, or exposing/selling such articles without licence. Remedies: injunction, damages/account of profits; criminal liability (fine) also possible.
- **Rights of the design holder**: exclusive right to apply the design to the article and to deal in such articles; enforceable against pirates for the full term.
- **Non-use**: Designs Act does NOT require working/use of the design (unlike some countries) — registration is not lost for non-use; however, compulsory licensing provisions exist in Sec 19 (rarely used).
- **Recent change**: Designs (Amendment) Rules 2021 — procedural streamlining, expedited examination option.

### Geographical Indication (GI): Definition and Concept

- **GI definition (Sec 2(1)(e), GI of Goods (Registration & Protection) Act, 1999)**: An indication which identifies goods as originating from a defined territory/region/country, **where a given quality, reputation, or other characteristic of the goods is essentially attributable to its geographical origin**. Examples: Darjeeling Tea, Kanchipuram Silk, Mysore Sandal Soap, Champagne, Roquefort cheese.
- **Nature of goods**: agricultural (Basmati, Alphonso Mango), natural (Madurai Sungudi), manufactured (Channapatna Toys, Feni), handicraft (Pochampalli Ikat), foodstuff (Kashmiri Saffron).
- **Key concept — origin linkage**: the link between quality/reputation and the place of origin is the core of GI. Three linkages: quality, reputation, or other characteristic essentially attributable to geography (climate, soil, traditional skill of local artisans).
- **Governing law**: Geographical Indications of Goods (Registration & Protection) Act, **1999**, Rules 2002; administered by the **GI Registry, Chennai**, under CGPDTM.
- **GI vs trademark**: GI identifies *origin/place-linked goods* (e.g., Darjeeling tea from that region); trademark identifies *commercial source* (producer brand). GI is owned by a collective/association; trademark can be owned by one person.
- **GI vs geographical indication protection in TRIPS**: GIs are a **TRIPS (WTO) mandated** IP right — Article 22 (general protection), Article 23 (additional protection for wines and spirits). India's Act implements TRIPS.

### GI Eligibility, Rights of Holders, and Registration Process

- **Eligibility to apply (Sec 11)**: any **association of persons, producers, organization, or authority** established by law representing the interests of producers of the concerned goods (e.g., Tea Board for Darjeeling tea); a **single producer cannot apply** — the applicant must represent a collective.
- **Who can register**: association of producers, organization, or authority established by law representing producers' interests.
- **Rights of GI holders** (PYQ 2023 Q9a):
  - **Exclusive right to use** the GI on the designated goods.
  - Right to **exclude unauthorized use** by others — no one else can use the indication for goods not originating from the region (or for similar goods).
  - Right to obtain **relief for infringement** (injunction, damages).
  - Right of a **registered proprietor** to license others (authorized users must register separately).
- **Registration duration**: **10 years** from registration; renewable indefinitely for 10-year periods (unlike designs/patents, GI can last forever as long as the indication survives and is renewed).
- **Registration process (PYQ 2023 Q9a — step list)**:
  1. File application (Form GI-1) with the **GI Registry, Chennai** — along with a statement of case: how the goods' quality/reputation is linked to the region, a geographical map of the territory, the class of goods (Nice/Locarno-style classification), particulars of producers.
  2. Examination by the GI Registry (publication of application).
  3. **Publication in the GI Journal**.
  4. **Opposition period: 3 months** (extendable) — any person may oppose.
  5. If unopposed/opposition dismissed → **registration**; certificate issued; entry in the GI Register.
  6. **Authorized users** (producers within the region) file Form GI-3 to register as users and get rights to use the GI.
- **Infringement of GI (Sec 21-22)**: unauthorized use of a registered GI, use of a false indication of origin ("misleading as to true origin"), and **passing off** of goods with a false geographical origin.
- **Examples of Indian GIs**: Darjeeling Tea (first GI registered in India, 2004), Basmati (contested — see case studies), Mysore Silk, Banarasi Sarees, Feni (Goa, first GI for liquor), Coorg Arabica Coffee.
- **International examples**: Champagne (France, sparkling wine from Champagne region), Scotch Whisky, Roquefort, Parma Ham.

### IP Case Studies: Turmeric, Neem, Basmati (the "Big Three")

- **Turmeric (Curcuma longa) patent case (1995-1997)** (PYQ 2023 Q9b):
  - **Background**: In 1995, two Indian-born US researchers at the University of Mississippi Medical Center (Suman K. Das and Hari Har P. Cohly) were granted **US Patent 5,401,504** for "Use of turmeric in wound healing" — claiming the use of turmeric powder as a wound-healing agent.
  - **Problem**: Turmeric's wound-healing property is **ancient, traditional Indian knowledge** — the patent wrongly claimed this as novel.
  - **Action**: **CSIR (Council of Scientific & Industrial Research, India)** filed a re-examination request with the USPTO, providing **ancient Sanskrit texts, Ayurvedic scriptures, and published literature** (including ancient texts and a 1953 paper) proving prior knowledge and use.
  - **Outcome**: USPTO **revoked the patent in 1997**, after 2 years of litigation, holding the invention lacked novelty (anticipated by prior art). CSIR spent about $10,000 in total — versus millions in litigation cost for the patentee.
  - **Significance**: Landmark case — established that **traditional knowledge is prior art**; led to the creation of India's **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** (2001, CSIR + AYUSH) to document traditional knowledge in 5-10 languages in patent-compatible format, preventing bio-piracy.
- **Neem (Azadirachta indica) patent case (1994-2005)** (PYQ 2023 Q9b):
  - **Background**: In 1994, the **European Patent Office (EPO)** granted **EP 0436257 B1** to the US company **WR Grace and USDA** for a method of controlling fungi on plants using neem oil extract (a stable neem oil emulsion). Neem's fungicidal use was traditional Indian knowledge.
  - **Action**: In 1995, a coalition — **Vandana Shiva (Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Ecology, India), Magda Aelvoet (MEP, Greenpeace)** — filed an **opposition** at the EPO.
  - **Outcome (2000)**: EPO **revoked the patent** (Opposition Division) for **lack of novelty** — the method was known and used in Indian agriculture before the patent. WR Grace appealed; in **2005 the EPO Technical Board of Appeal upheld the revocation**, final.
  - **Significance**: First major victory against bio-piracy at EPO; proved that oral traditional knowledge (prior public use in India) defeats European novelty; high point of the anti-bio-piracy movement.
- **Basmati rice patent case (1997-2001)** (PYQ 2024 Q9b):
  - **Background**: In 1997, the US company **RiceTec Inc.** was granted **US Patent 5,663,484** for "Basmati rice lines and grains" — claiming novel rice lines and, importantly, grain characteristics similar to **traditional Indian/Pakistani Basmati** (long slender grains, aroma, starch characteristics). RiceTec also tried to trademark "Texmati" style names ("Basmati" itself was in the trademark claims).
  - **Problem**: Basmati is a **geographical indication of the Indian subcontinent** — RiceTec's patent and trademark claims threatened Indian Basmati export market (worth ~$300 million/yr) and could have restricted US market access.
  - **Action**: The **Government of India (Agricultural & Processed Food Products Export Development Authority — APEDA)** and Indian industry filed a **re-examination request** at USPTO; supported by the rice research community; India also began the GI registration process for Basmati.
  - **Outcome**: In 2001, USPTO **re-examined and allowed amended claims**: RiceTec was forced to drop 20 of the original 22 claims; only **3 narrow claims** remained (specific cross-bred rice lines, not generic Basmati). RiceTec also withdrew its "Basmati" trademark attempt (though "Texmati"/"Jasmati" style names remained — India later pursued separate challenges).
  - **Significance**: Protected India's **rice export market**; highlighted that **biotech patent claims can be narrowed via re-examination**; catalyzed the GI registration of Basmati (contested between India and Pakistan — the matter of GI in EU remains ongoing; India filed Basmati GI application in 2008; long dispute with Pakistan resolved 2014 by agreement to jointly pursue EU GI).
- **Other landmark IP litigation in India** (brief notes):
  - **Novartis vs Union of India (2013)**: Supreme Court refused patent for **Glivec (imatinib mesylate beta-crystalline form)** under Sec 3(d) — no enhanced efficacy shown; upheld India's strict patentability for incremental drug modifications. Landmark for access to medicines.
  - **Bajaj Auto vs TVS Motor (2009-2012)**: patent infringement suit over **twin-spark plug engine technology** — TVS's use held infringing in some claims; illustrative of engineering patent litigation in India.
  - **Roche vs Cipla (2012)**: Delhi HC — Cipla's generic **erlotinib (Tarceva)** did not infringe Roche's process claims; notable for balancing patent rights vs affordable medicines.
  - **Enercon vs Enercon India**: dispute over trademark/goodwill of the Enercon brand (wind energy).
  - **Dabur vs Emami** / **Parle vs Britannia**: look-alike packaging / passing off cases.
  - **RiceTec** (above), **Monsanto Bt Cotton** royalty disputes (trademark + patent licensing in seeds).

### Common Themes across the Big Three (exam insight)

- All three involved **bio-piracy** — patenting of traditional/indigenous knowledge by foreign entities.
- All were defeated using **prior art** — published traditional knowledge defeats novelty.
- All three outcomes created lasting change: TKDL (Turmeric), stronger anti-bio-piracy jurisprudence (Neem), and GI protection movement (Basmati).
- These cases justify why **Section 3(o) of the Indian Patents Act** excludes traditional knowledge from patentability, and why India built the **TKDL** and the **GI Act 1999**.

- Design = appearance only, judged by eye; Designs Act 2000; term 10 + 5 = 15 years max; statement of novelty + representations.
- GI = quality/reputation essentially attributable to geographical origin; GI Act 1999; GI Registry Chennai; term 10 years renewable; only collectives apply; authorized users.
- Turmeric: US patent 5,401,504 revoked by USPTO (1997) on CSIR's re-examination — TKDL born.
- Neem: EPO patent of WR Grace/USDA revoked (2000, upheld 2005) — opposition by Vandana Shiva/Greenpeace.
- Basmati: RiceTec US patent 5,663,484 narrowed from 22 to 3 claims (2001) — APEDA re-examination; GI protection push.
